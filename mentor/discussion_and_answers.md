# Discussion prompts and indicative answers
## Simulation
Are atoms in a solid stationary? Usually they vibrate around persistent local positions.
Does faster motion imply liquid? No; motion and neighbour rearrangement must be examined.
What remains fixed in our comparison? Particle number, box size, model, boundary rules
and observation protocol. The preparation and energy intervention are recorded.
Why wait after adding energy? The intervention creates a transient; waiting reduces
its immediate influence, but does not prove equilibrium.
Why can two repeats differ? Different initial velocities, finite sampling and sensitivity
to initial conditions. Compare protocols and fluctuations before calling one incorrect.
Why might cooling fail to restore the initial pattern? Finite-time history and barriers;
this observation alone neither establishes irreversibility nor an equilibrium transition.

## Reading
Schroeder: the model represents particles through forces and numerical integration;
its two-dimensional geometry and pair potential are assumptions. Students may cite
other specific assumptions if they explain their consequences.
Olah: knowing rain can help predict clothing; independence means the distribution of
one variable does not change when the other is known. Many paired observations are
needed to estimate a joint distribution. One observation gives no frequency law.
Levitt: models simplify complex systems; compare predictions with appropriate external
observations or known limiting cases. A convincing animation is not sufficient validation.

## Colab
A notebook combines narrative, code and saved output. Running out of order can leave
hidden variables. Restart and run all checks reproducibility. Saving the notebook
is different from saving runtime files or submitting its link to the mentor.
Accept alternative explanations that identify the same mechanism. Ask students to
point to their evidence and identify what would change their minds.
